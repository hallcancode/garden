An _ideal gas_ is a [[Introduction to Gases|gas]] that follows the five postulates of Kinetic Molecular Theory, that is:

1. A gas is composed of a large number of particles that are in constant random motion.  
2. The distance between gas particles is much greater than the size of the particles, so the volumes of the particles are negligible.
3. Interparticle interactions are so weak that they are negligible.  
4. The particles collide with one another and the surfaces of their container, but all collisions are perfectly elastic, meaning they do not change the average kinetic energy of the particles.  
5. The average kinetic energy of all particles of a gas depends only on the temperature, and at any given temperature all molecules have the exact same kinetic energy.  

## The Ideal Gas Law
The *ideal gas law* is the relationship between the amount of matter, temperature, pressure, and volume of a gaseous system, such that they are all proportional to one another:
$$
\begin{align}
V &= \frac{nRT}{P}  \\
PV &= nRT
\end{align}
$$
Where $V$ is the volume, $n$ is the number of moles, $T$ is the temperature, $P$ is the pressure, and $R$ is the gas constant.  The gas constant's value greatly depends on the units of the other measurements, and is often converted between several units, however in liter atmospheres per a kelvin mole as well as joules per a kelvin mole it is measured as:
$$
\begin{align}
R &= 0.08206 \text{ L} \cdot \text{atm} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1} \\
&= 8.3145 \text{ J} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1}
\end{align}
$$
The Ideal Gas Law is a combination of three other laws: Boyle's law, Charle's law, and Avogadro's law.  Each of these individual laws states how the quantities of the Ideal Gas Law are related to one another, often individually.
