Each [[Logical Connectives|logical connective]] has a [[Introduction to Sets|set-theoretic]] equivalent.  In fact, the logical connectives are generally believed to come from set theory.  

## Union
The *union* between two sets is the set containing all the elements of both sets.  The union can be thought of as the set-theory equivalent of the logical connective "or." An union of two sets, $A$ and $B$, may also be defined formally in set-builder notation as:
$$A \cup B = \left\{x| \left(x \in A\right) \lor \left(x \in B\right) \right\}$$

## Intersection
the *intersection* between two sets is the set of elements that both sets contain.  The intersection can be thought as the as the equivalent of the logical connective "and" for set-theory.  An intersection of the sets $A$ and $B$ may also be defined formally in set-builder notation as:
$$A \cap B = \left\{\left(x \in A\right) \land \left(x \in B\right)\right\}$$

## Complement
The *complement* of a set is the set of elements not contained within the set, but are contained within the universal set.  The complement could be though of as the set-theoretic equivalent of the "negation" logical connective.  A complement of a set of $A$ could be written as:
$$A^\prime = \left\{x \in U | \neg \left(x \in A\right)\right\}$$
Where $U$ is the universal set.
