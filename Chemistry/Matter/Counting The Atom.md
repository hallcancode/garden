Due to the incredibly small nature of the [[The Atom|atom]], the human eye can not possibly count them efficiently.  This is why chemists use the [[Atomic & Molecular Mass|molecular mass]] to calculate the number of atoms with a particular sample.  

Atoms are counted in mole atoms (officially $\text{mol} \cdot \text{atoms}$, but typically just $\text{mol}$), moles are the [[SI Units|SI unit]] for the amount of a substance.  Moles are defined as the number of carbon ($\ce{C}$) atoms with a 12 gram sample of Carbon-12, this number is known as Avogadro’s constant, $N_a$: 
$$
\begin{align}
N_a&=6.02214179\times10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}\\
&\approx6.022\times10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}
\end{align}
$$
Moles simply act as a multiplier, much like the word “dozen,” and they can be used to count anything.  The number of atom moles $N$ is proportional to the mass of the sample $m$ and molecular mass $M$, such that:
$$
m \times\frac{1\text{ mol}}{M} = N
$$
Where the molecular mass is measured in grams per a mole.
