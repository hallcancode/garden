*Recreation ecology* is the study of the impacts that wild-land recreation has.  Impacts generally refer to the negative ones that humans have on the natural resources, but it can also be positive.  

For an example, benefits include: 

 - **Economic value.**  Over the last year outdoor recreation on national land has contributed around $55 billion dollars.  
 - Workforce development
 - **Ecosystem services.**  Services that aren’t necessarily economic, but that we still need.  

However, the increased interest in recreation also brings new issues.  For an example:

 - **Increased pressure on wildland-urban interface.**  Erosion from MTB trails steering fish away from a specific location.  
 - **Risk of disturbance.**  Such as wildfire.  
 - Carrying capacity

Let’s dive into ways of mitigating these issues.  With more trail networks, and more load on the trails being used, new fields of research are emerging: 

 - Soil compaction
 - Erosion monitoring
 - Water quality

Places being quite is very important for recreation as well.  This loops back to my idea of “different silences.”  View shed analysis can hide things from the people who use a park see, in the Great Smokey Mountains there is a literal highway passing through that is managed not to be seen.  

Through complaints and monitoring we can measure the amount of negative impact.  This data can then be used to influence legislation, land management practices, and public perceptions.  We can also do land-based monitoring:  

 - Soil penetrometer: tests how dense the soil is and how much water is can absorb.  
 - Hydraulic conductivity: how much water the Earth can absorb (like rain.)

## Social-Ecological Systems Framework (SES)
Managing both the sociological and ecological systems, through action interventions (i.e., logging, fishing, etc.) and ecosystem services (i.e., fuel, fiber, food, water, etc.)
